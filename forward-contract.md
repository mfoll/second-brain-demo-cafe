# Forward contract

## Summary

A forward is a private agreement between two parties to exchange an asset at a set price on a future date. Unlike futures, forwards are negotiated directly rather than traded on an exchange and usually have no regular margin payments, so gains and losses build up until settlement. They are used to hedge, most often currency risk, or to speculate, and they carry counterparty risk: if one side fails to deliver, the other may lose heavily.

## Why It Matters

Read two days after Glenvarrow's offer ([[glenvarrow-meeting-2026-10-06]]). Glenvarrow's fixed price is a forward in all but name: a privately agreed price for butter delivered over six months. The article's two points that mattered for us were hedging (we want a known cost, not a bet) and counterparty risk, which for a bakery means the dairy's reliability more than its finances. That is why Coldbeck's delivery record matters as much as its price ([[should-we-sign-the-fixed-price-butter-contract]]).

## Key Details

- Private and customised, as Moira's shortfall clause shows.
- No margin payments: the gain or loss against the market shows up only over the term.
